# Chapter 11: Zen and Chan

---

There is a famous story about Bodhidharma, the Indian monk traditionally credited with bringing Chan Buddhism to China in the 5th or 6th century CE. The Emperor Wu of Liang, a great patron of Buddhism, met with Bodhidharma and proudly listed his accomplishments: the temples he'd built, the monks he'd supported, the sutras he'd commissioned. "What merit have I accumulated?" he asked.

"None whatsoever," Bodhidharma said.

The emperor, flustered, tried another angle. "What is the highest truth of the holy teachings?"

"Vast emptiness, nothing holy."

"Then who is this standing before me?"

"I don't know."

This story may be apocryphal — most of the early Chan patriarchs are more legend than history — but it captures something essential about the tradition: its refusal of spiritual achievement as a category, its suspicion of elaborate doctrine, its directness that can feel almost rude. Zen is Buddhism that has been compressed until only the sharpest edge remains.

---

## Chan in China

Chan (the Chinese transliteration of the Sanskrit dhyana, meaning meditation) developed over several centuries in China, emerging as a distinct school somewhere around the 7th to 9th centuries CE. It drew on Indian Buddhist meditation teachings — particularly those concerned with direct insight into the nature of mind — and fused them with the Chinese sensibility shaped by Daoism and Confucianism.

What makes Chan distinctive is its claim that awakening is not something to be built up gradually through accumulating merit, study, and practice. Awakening is the natural state of mind, already present, always available. The problem is not that you lack it; the problem is that you keep looking for it as though it were somewhere else. As one Chan master put it: "Before enlightenment, chop wood and carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood and carry water."

This is not a teaching that sitting meditation is pointless. Chan monasteries had grueling practice schedules. It's a teaching about what you're looking for when you sit — and what you think "finding it" would mean. If you're sitting in order to acquire some special state, you've already gone wrong. The practice is to stop acquiring and start seeing.

## The Patriarchs

Chan tradition tells the story of its founding through a lineage of awakened masters: Bodhidharma, who famously sat facing a wall for nine years; the Second through Fifth Patriarchs; and then the Sixth Patriarch, Huineng, whose Platform Sutra is one of the most important texts in Chinese Buddhism.

Huineng was — legend says — illiterate, a poor woodcutter from the south. When the Fifth Patriarch Hongren announced that he would transmit the robe and bowl (the symbols of succession) to whoever demonstrated the deepest understanding, the head monk Shenxiu wrote a verse on the wall:

*The body is the Bodhi tree,*
*The mind like a bright mirror's stand.*
*Polish it diligently at all times,*
*Let no dust alight.*

Huineng had someone read it to him. Then he had his own verse written in response:

*Bodhi has no tree,*
*Nor has the bright mirror any stand.*
*Originally there is not a single thing —*
*Where could dust alight?*

Hongren gave Huineng the transmission. The tradition was now split between the gradual school (polish the mirror) and the sudden school (there is no mirror to polish, and never was). The sudden school — Huineng's lineage — won, historically, and its spirit pervades what we now call Zen.

## Koans

One of the most distinctive practices of the Rinzai school of Zen (one of the two main Japanese schools) is koan practice. A koan is a question or statement given by a teacher to a student that cannot be resolved by ordinary logical thinking.

The most famous: "What is the sound of one hand clapping?"

Or: "What was your original face before your parents were born?"

Or the simplest and arguably most profound: "Mu." (A monk asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature. Zhaozhou answers: "Mu" — which means "no" or "nothing," but in context is not simply a negative. The student is told to investigate this word until they have fully resolved it.)

Koans are not riddles with clever answers. They are designed to exhaust the conceptual mind — to bring a practitioner to the edge of what ordinary thinking can do, and then past it. When the conceptual mind gives up, something else opens. The teacher then tests whether the opening is genuine or just another clever thought.

Koan practice requires a teacher who has worked through the koans themselves and can recognize authentic resolution. This is one reason the teacher-student relationship in Zen (called *dokusan* or *sanzen*, private interview) is so central. You can't fake your way through it — not with a teacher who knows.

## Soto: Just Sitting

The other major Japanese school, Soto Zen, takes a different approach. Associated with the 13th-century master Dōgen, Soto emphasizes *shikantaza* — "just sitting." No koan to solve. No goal to achieve. Simply sit, fully present, and that is the practice.

Dōgen's writing — collected in the *Shobogenzo* ("Treasury of the True Dharma Eye") — is among the most extraordinary philosophical literature in Buddhism. Dense, paradoxical, poetic, demanding: it circles the same insights from dozens of angles, refusing to let any formulation settle into dogma.

His instruction for zazen (sitting meditation) is precise and specific: posture, hand position, eyes slightly open and cast downward, not falling asleep, not lost in thought. But his understanding of what is happening during that sitting is vast. "To study the Buddha-way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be actualized by the ten thousand things." Sitting is not preparation for awakening. Sitting, done correctly, is awakening expressing itself.

## Zen Culture

One of the most remarkable things about Zen is the way its sensibility permeated Japanese culture far beyond the monastery walls.

The tea ceremony, *ikebana* (flower arranging), *sumi-e* (ink painting), *haiku* poetry, the aesthetics of the Zen garden — all of these bear the imprint of the Zen emphasis on simplicity, presence, and the direct expression of what is. The Japanese concept of *mono no aware* — the bittersweet sense of the transience of things — is Buddhist impermanence made into an aesthetic.

Zen swordsmanship. Zen archery. Zen cooking. The idea that any activity, done with full presence and no gap between self and action, can be a vehicle for the same awareness that zazen cultivates. The martial arts and the arts of daily life become practice.

## Zen in the West

We met D.T. Suzuki and Shunryu Suzuki in Chapter 9. To their influence add Philip Kapleau, whose book *The Three Pillars of Zen* (1965) was the first to give Western readers a direct, practical account of what actually happens in a Zen retreat. Add Thich Nhat Hanh, the Vietnamese Zen monk who blended Zen practice with engaged social activism and wrote with a clarity and warmth that has reached millions of readers. Add the Dharma bums, the Beat poets, the long chain of Westerners who sat in Zen centers from San Francisco to Vermont to London and found something they couldn't name but couldn't let go of.

Western Zen is its own phenomenon now — shaped by its Asian roots but genuinely transformed by the encounter with Western psychology, gender equality, democratic institutional forms, and a culture that is both more psychologically sophisticated and less monastically inclined than traditional Zen's home context.

The question it keeps returning to — the question at the heart of the tradition — hasn't changed: What is this? Right here, right now. What is actually happening?
