# Chapter 4: Early Buddhism and the Pali Canon

---

The Buddha taught for roughly 45 years after that first discourse at Sarnath. He walked the roads of the Ganges plain, sat under trees, ate in people's homes, and talked with everyone — kings and outcasts, merchants and courtesans, farmers and philosophers. He answered questions, corrected misunderstandings, adapted his teaching to whoever was in front of him.

He wrote nothing down.

This was not unusual for the time. In ancient India, the most sacred knowledge was transmitted orally — memorized, recited, passed from teacher to student in an unbroken chain. Writing was for merchants and administrators. The Dhamma was meant to be heard, chanted, and held in the body and mind of the community.

What happened after the Buddha died — around 483 BCE, in a town called Kushinagar, at the age of 80 — is one of the most important and underappreciated chapters in religious history.

---

## The First Council

Shortly after the Buddha's death, a gathering of around 500 senior monks convened at Rajagaha. Their purpose: to recite and confirm everything the Buddha had taught, before memory faded and versions diverged.

Two monks took center stage.

Ananda had been the Buddha's personal attendant for the last 25 years of his life. He had heard nearly every discourse the Buddha gave, and his memory was legendary. He recited the teachings — the suttas — from memory, beginning each one with the phrase that has opened Buddhist scriptures ever since: *"Thus have I heard..."*

Upali recited the Vinaya — the code of monastic discipline, the rules governing the community's daily life.

The assembly listened, debated, and confirmed. This is the tradition's account of how the Pali Canon began: not as a text written by one person, but as a communal act of remembrance.

---

## What the Canon Contains

The Pali Canon — sometimes called the Tipitaka, or "Three Baskets" — is vast. If printed in English, it would run to dozens of volumes. It is organized into three sections:

**The Vinaya Pitaka** covers monastic rules and their origins. Every rule comes with a story — the situation that prompted the Buddha to establish it. Reading the Vinaya is like watching a community figure itself out in real time: what happens when monks argue, when laypeople complain, when edge cases arise that no one anticipated.

**The Sutta Pitaka** contains the discourses — thousands of teachings, conversations, and narratives attributed to the Buddha and his senior disciples. These are organized into five collections (Nikayas) by length and type. The Majjhima Nikaya (Middle Length Discourses) and the Digha Nikaya (Long Discourses) are among the most widely read today. The Dhammapada — a short collection of verses — has probably been translated into more languages than any other Buddhist text.

**The Abhidhamma Pitaka** is the philosophical and psychological analysis: a systematic attempt to map every mental state, every moment of consciousness, every factor of experience into a precise taxonomy. It's the most technical section, and the most debated — some traditions treat it as the Buddha's own word, others as a later scholarly elaboration.

## The Second Council and the First Split

About a hundred years after the first council, another gathering was called — this one less harmonious.

The issue was monastic discipline. A group of monks in the eastern region of Vaishali had adopted ten practices that stricter monks considered violations of the Vinaya. Among them: accepting gold and silver (money), eating after noon, and some looser rules around communal meals.

The conservative faction said: these are violations, full stop. The eastern monks said: times change, contexts differ, these practices are reasonable adaptations.

The assembly ruled against the Vaishali monks. But the dispute didn't end there. The community fractured. This was the beginning of what scholars call the "eighteen schools" of early Buddhism — different communities, spread across different regions, gradually developing their own versions of the canon, their own interpretations of doctrine, their own approaches to practice.

Most of these schools are gone now, leaving only fragments and references. But one of them survives intact: the Theravada school, which preserved the Pali Canon and carried it south and east, eventually becoming the dominant form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos.

---

## The Question of the Canon's Age

Here's something worth sitting with: the Pali Canon as a written text wasn't committed to writing until around the 1st century BCE — roughly 400 years after the Buddha died. Before that, it was oral.

This makes modern historians nervous. How accurate can an oral tradition be over four centuries? How much changed, evolved, was added?

The honest answer is: we don't fully know. What we can say is that oral cultures in ancient India were extraordinarily rigorous about memorization — the Brahmin tradition had been preserving the Vedas orally for over a thousand years with remarkable consistency. The monastic communities had strong motivation to preserve the teachings accurately. And where we can cross-check — comparing the Pali Canon with parallel texts preserved in Chinese translation from different early schools — the core teachings align with striking consistency.

This doesn't mean every word is the Buddha's word. Scholars have identified layers of the canon that appear to be later elaborations. The framework of Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, dependent origination, the major meditation teachings — these appear consistently enough, across enough sources, that most scholars treat them as early and likely authentic.

What we have in the Pali Canon is not a transcript. It's something more like the distilled memory of a community — imperfect, shaped by transmission, but oriented toward preserving something real.

---

## The Living Quality of Early Buddhism

One thing that comes through clearly in the early texts is how alive and responsive early Buddhism was. The Buddha was not handing down a fixed system. He was talking to people — this monk struggling with anger, that layperson worried about death, a grieving mother, a hostile Brahmin, a king asking about statecraft.

His answers varied. He taught differently to different people, emphasized different practices for different temperaments, and was willing to revise, reframe, and adapt. The tradition even has a word for this: *upaya*, skillful means — the ability to meet each person where they are.

That quality — pragmatic, responsive, aimed at actual liberation rather than doctrinal correctness — is what the early texts keep returning to. The teaching is a raft, the Buddha famously said. You use it to cross the river. When you've crossed, you don't carry the raft on your head.
