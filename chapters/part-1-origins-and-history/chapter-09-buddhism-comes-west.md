# Chapter 9: Buddhism Comes West

---

Buddhism has been coming West for longer than most people realize.

Alexander the Great's campaigns brought Greek soldiers to the borders of the Indian subcontinent in the 4th century BCE. The Greek kingdoms that remained in Bactria (modern-day Afghanistan) after his death produced a remarkable cultural hybrid: Greco-Buddhist art, in which the Buddha was depicted in the style of Apollo, and the philosophy of the Gandharan region absorbed influences from both Greek and Indian thought. The first Buddha images we know of were made in this world, by artists who looked to Greek sculpture for their models.

Ashoka sent missionaries westward toward Egypt and the Mediterranean. There is ongoing scholarly debate about whether early Christian ascetic communities — particularly the Therapeutae in Egypt, described by Philo of Alexandria in the 1st century CE — showed Buddhist influence. Nothing is proven. But the exchange of ideas along the ancient trade routes was more extensive than our historical categories usually capture.

None of this, however, is what we mean when we talk about Buddhism coming West. That story really begins in the 19th century.

---

## The Orientalists

In the early decades of the 1800s, European scholars began, for the first time, to seriously study the languages and texts of the Buddhist world. The discovery of Sanskrit's relationship to Greek and Latin had created enormous enthusiasm for Indian thought among European intellectuals. Men like Eugène Burnouf in France and T.W. Rhys Davids in Britain began translating Pali and Sanskrit Buddhist texts, making them available to a Western audience for the first time.

The Buddha they constructed was shaped by what their culture valued — and what it was anxious about. Victorian Europe was grappling with the challenges Darwin posed to Christian faith, with rising materialism and scientific determinism, with the sense that the old certainties were dissolving. The Buddha they found in the Pali Canon looked, to many of them, like a rational philosopher, a spiritual empiricist, a man who had rejected ritual and dogma in favor of direct inquiry. He was, conveniently, a mirror of what thoughtful Westerners admired.

This was a partial reading, and it missed much. But it was also, in some ways, genuinely responsive to something in the early texts — the Buddha's insistence on verification, his resistance to mere belief, his focus on direct experience. The Victorian reading exaggerated these features, but it didn't invent them.

---

## The 1893 Parliament

A watershed moment in the Western encounter with Buddhism came at the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago.

The Ceylonese (Sri Lankan) monk Anagarika Dharmapala and the Japanese Zen master Soyen Shaku both spoke to packed audiences of Westerners who had never encountered living Buddhist teachers. Their reception was electric. Dharmapala's English was fluent and his framing was sharp — he presented Buddhism not as an exotic foreign religion but as a rational, scientific philosophy of mind fully compatible with modern thought.

Among those in the audience was a young journalist named Paul Carus, who would go on to publish one of the most widely read introductions to Buddhism in American history. In the audience also, or in contact with those who were, were threads that would eventually lead to the Beats, the countercultural movements of the 1960s, and the mindfulness revolution of the late 20th century.

---

## Zen Comes to America

The most significant early transmission of a living Buddhist practice tradition to the West came through Zen.

D.T. Suzuki — a Japanese scholar who had studied at the monastery of Engakuji — began publishing in English in the early 20th century, and by midcentury had introduced Zen thought to an enormous Western audience. His books were read by John Cage, Thomas Merton, Carl Jung, and the Beat generation writers. Jack Kerouac's *The Dharma Bums* and Allen Ginsberg's poetry were soaked in the Zen aesthetic — spontaneous, present, suspicious of fixed ideas, in love with the direct and the concrete.

But Suzuki's Zen was largely literary and philosophical. The transmission of actual practice — sitting, koan work, dokusan with a teacher — came through a different channel.

In 1959, Shunryu Suzuki (no relation to D.T.) arrived in San Francisco to serve the small Japanese-American Buddhist congregation at Sokoji Temple. He expected a brief posting. He ended up staying for the rest of his life. Americans started showing up to sit zazen with him — people with no connection to the Japanese-American community, just people drawn to the practice. He taught them, and eventually founded San Francisco Zen Center, one of the most significant Zen institutions outside Asia.

His talks, collected in the book *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind*, remain one of the most beautiful introductions to Zen practice in any language.

---

## The Tibetan Diaspora

In 1959, following China's military occupation of Tibet and a failed uprising against Chinese rule, the Dalai Lama and roughly 80,000 Tibetan refugees fled across the Himalayas into India and Nepal. The diaspora that followed would, unexpectedly, accelerate the transmission of Tibetan Buddhism to the West.

Tibetan teachers who might never have left Tibet now found themselves in a new world with students hungry to learn. Chögyam Trungpa arrived in the United States in 1970 and founded Naropa University in Boulder, Colorado — the first Buddhist-inspired university in North America. Kalu Rinpoche, Sogyal Rinpoche, Pema Chödrön, and dozens of other teachers brought Tibetan practices to Western students who received three-year retreat trainings, empowerments, and direct teachings that had previously been available only in Asia.

The 14th Dalai Lama himself became the most recognized Buddhist figure in the world — a Nobel Peace Prize laureate whose teachings on compassion, on the relationship between Buddhism and science, and on the nature of mind have reached millions of people who will never formally practice Buddhism.

---

## The Mindfulness Revolution

Perhaps the most far-reaching Western adaptation of Buddhism has been the mindfulness movement.

In the late 1970s, a molecular biologist named Jon Kabat-Zinn was on retreat at the Insight Meditation Society in Barre, Massachusetts — one of the first Theravada practice centers in America, founded by teachers including Jack Kornfield and Joseph Goldstein who had trained with masters in Thailand and Burma. On retreat, Kabat-Zinn had an insight: the core practices of Vipassana meditation could be stripped of their Buddhist framework and introduced in a secular context to help people with chronic pain and stress.

He developed Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), offered it through the University of Massachusetts Medical School, and watched as the results attracted serious clinical and scientific attention. MBSR became the template for evidence-based mindfulness interventions that are now used in hospitals, schools, corporations, prisons, and clinics around the world.

The mindfulness movement is genuinely contested within Buddhist communities. Some teachers see it as a welcome broadening of access — if these practices reduce suffering, good, regardless of whether participants identify as Buddhists. Others worry that stripping the practices from their ethical and philosophical framework produces something that looks like meditation but lacks the transformative depth of the full path — "McMindfulness," a stress-management tool rather than a vehicle for liberation.

Both concerns are legitimate. What's not in dispute is the scale of the impact: estimates suggest that tens of millions of people in the West now have some regular mindfulness practice, most of them with no formal Buddhist identity. The seeds Ashoka sent westward took two thousand years to germinate, but they are unmistakably in the ground.

---

## Where We Are Now

Western Buddhism in the early 21st century is a genuinely new development — not just Asian Buddhism transplanted to new soil, but something being created in real time through the encounter between ancient practices and modern conditions.

This encounter has already transformed both sides. Western practitioners have pressed Buddhist communities on questions of teacher accountability, gender equity, and psychological safety that many traditional institutions were not structured to address. At the same time, Buddhist philosophy and practice have entered serious conversation with neuroscience, psychology, philosophy of mind, and contemplative research in ways that are producing new knowledge in both directions.

The integration is not complete. It may never be complete — Buddhism has always been in process, always adapting. What we can say with confidence is that the tradition is alive, that it is changing, and that the questions it has always asked — about suffering, about the nature of mind, about how to live — are no less urgent now than they were in the forests of the Ganges plain 2,500 years ago.
