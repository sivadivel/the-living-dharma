# Chapter 8: Buddhism Moves East

---

In 268 BCE, a young king named Ashoka came to power over the Maurya Empire — an empire that, by the time of his reign, covered most of the Indian subcontinent. He was ambitious, militaristic, and brutal in the way of ancient conquerors.

Then, around 261 BCE, he conquered the kingdom of Kalinga. The campaign was successful. It was also catastrophic. The ancient edicts Ashoka later had carved into rock pillars across his empire describe what he saw: a hundred thousand killed, many more deported, suffering on a massive scale. And Ashoka — the man who had ordered all of it — found himself unable to bear what he had done.

He converted to Buddhism.

What followed was one of the most consequential acts of royal patronage in religious history. Ashoka didn't just become a Buddhist privately. He reoriented the entire apparatus of his empire toward Dhamma — toward ethics, non-harm, compassion, and the spread of the teachings. He built hospitals for humans and animals. He planted shade trees along roads. He sent missionaries — including, tradition says, his own son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta — to carry the teachings to Sri Lanka, to Burma, to the edges of the Hellenistic world.

Buddhism was no longer a regional movement along the Ganges plain. It was a world religion in the making.

---

## The Silk Road

The route that would carry Buddhism into Central Asia and China was already ancient by the time the Dharma began traveling it. The Silk Road — really a network of routes rather than a single road — connected the Mediterranean world with China along corridors through Persia, Afghanistan, and the oases of Central Asia.

Buddhist monks were among the most important travelers on this road, not as merchants but as translators of culture. They walked with trade caravans, established monasteries at oasis towns like Dunhuang and Turfan, and slowly, over centuries, transformed the spiritual landscape of the entire region.

The challenge they faced was formidable: China had its own ancient and sophisticated civilization, its own philosophical traditions — Confucianism and Daoism — its own deeply rooted assumptions about how the world worked. Buddhism wasn't walking into a spiritual vacuum. It was entering a conversation already in progress.

---

## Buddhism in China

Buddhism first arrived in China around the 1st century CE, during the Han Dynasty. The early translators faced an immediate problem: how do you render a language as foreign as Sanskrit — with its elaborate grammatical structure and its enormous technical vocabulary — into classical Chinese?

The early solution was *geyi*, or "matching concepts" — using existing Chinese philosophical terms as rough equivalents for Buddhist ones. The concept of *dao* (the Way, in Daoism) was used for *dharma*. The concept of *wu wei* (non-action) was used to approximate aspects of nirvana. This worked well enough to make Buddhism comprehensible to Chinese readers, but it also introduced significant distortions — Buddhist and Daoist concepts are not the same, and translating one using the vocabulary of the other created confusions that took centuries to untangle.

The great translator Kumarajiva, a Central Asian monk who worked in China in the early 5th century CE, began a new era. He trained a team of scholar-monks and produced translations of such clarity and literary beauty that many remain definitive in Chinese Buddhism to this day. His translation of the Diamond Sutra and the Lotus Sutra became foundational texts of Chinese Buddhism.

Over the following centuries, Chinese Buddhism developed its own schools — adapted forms of Indian schools but transformed by Chinese culture, values, and needs. The most influential would be Chan and Pure Land, which we'll explore in detail in Part II.

## Korea and Japan

From China, Buddhism moved to Korea in the 4th century CE, where it was officially adopted by each of the three kingdoms in succession: Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla. Korean monks traveled to China to study, brought back texts and practices, and developed their own distinctive synthesis. The Korean tradition of Son Buddhism — the Korean form of Chan — would produce its own remarkable teachers and its own approach to awakening.

Japan received Buddhism in the 6th century CE, primarily through Korea, though Chinese influence was also direct and substantial. The traditional date — 552 CE — marks an official transmission: the Korean king of Baekje sent Buddhist texts and images to the Japanese emperor as a diplomatic gift. The regent Prince Shotoku became one of Buddhism's most powerful patrons in Japanese history, writing commentaries on sutras and building temples that still stand.

Japanese Buddhism would develop perhaps the most diverse and creative forms of the tradition outside India. Tendai, Shingon, Pure Land, Zen, Nichiren — each school would leave its mark on Japanese culture so deeply that it is almost impossible to understand Japanese art, literature, aesthetics, or ethics without understanding the Buddhism that shaped them.

## The Body of Evidence at Dunhuang

In 1900, a Daoist monk named Wang Yuanlu was doing maintenance work in a complex of Buddhist cave temples near Dunhuang, an oasis town on the edge of the Gobi Desert. He discovered a walled-up chamber containing over 40,000 manuscripts — scrolls in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, and a dozen other languages, dating back to the 5th century CE.

The Dunhuang manuscripts are one of the great archaeological treasures of the modern era. They tell us an enormous amount about how Buddhism actually lived — not just in great monasteries but in ordinary communities, as practiced by people whose names we'll never know. There are translations in progress, requests for prayers, liturgical calendars, personal correspondence, texts studied by monks in remote outposts far from any urban center.

The Dunhuang cache reminds us that the spread of Buddhism across Asia was not just a story of emperors and great masters. It was also a story of ordinary people carrying a lamp from town to town, village to village, generation to generation.

## What Happened to Buddhism in India

There is a painful irony in the story of Buddhism's expansion: as it spread across Asia, it was slowly dying in the land of its birth.

The reasons are complex and disputed. The revival of Hindu traditions under the Gupta Empire drew royal patronage away from Buddhist monasteries. The invasions of the Huns in the 5th and 6th centuries CE destroyed institutions and disrupted networks. And in the 12th century, the Muslim Ghurid invasions dealt a decisive blow — the great monastic universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila were sacked and burned, their monk-scholars killed or scattered.

Buddhism never recovered its earlier strength in India. Today, it represents only a small percentage of the Indian population — though the 20th-century conversion movement led by B.R. Ambedkar, who brought millions of Dalits into Buddhism as an act of liberation from caste oppression, created a significant new community.

What survived in India was carried east and preserved in the traditions that had received it — Tibet, Sri Lanka, China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia. Buddhism became, paradoxically, better preserved outside its homeland than within it.
