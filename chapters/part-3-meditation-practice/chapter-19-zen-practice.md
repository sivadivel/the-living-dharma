# Chapter 19: Zen Practice

---

Zen is famous for being impossible to explain. Teachers throughout the centuries have gone to sometimes absurd lengths to demonstrate this — shouting, hitting students with sticks, holding up a flower and saying nothing, responding to deep philosophical questions with apparent non-sequiturs. The tradition seems almost to enjoy being inexplicable.

And yet Zen practice itself — what you actually do — is in many ways quite simple. You sit. You walk. You work. You stay present.

The challenge is not the complexity of the instructions. The challenge is the simplicity. Most of us are expert at complexity. We are novices at just sitting.

---

## Zazen

The heart of Zen practice is zazen — "just sitting." The instruction is almost absurdly stripped down: sit in a stable posture, hold still, stay present.

Posture matters more in Zen than in some other traditions. The traditional forms — full lotus, half lotus, Burmese position (both feet on the floor), seiza (kneeling on a bench) — are designed to create physical stability and upright alertness. The spine is straight but not rigid. The chin is slightly tucked. The eyes are half open, cast downward at about a 45-degree angle. Hands are held in the cosmic mudra: left hand resting on right, thumbs lightly touching, forming an oval.

These details are not arbitrary. Zen has always understood that body and mind are not separate — that how you hold your body shapes the quality of your mind, and that sitting in the same posture as the generations before you creates a kind of continuity with the tradition itself.

In Soto Zen, you face the wall. Historically this was associated with Bodhidharma, who sat facing a wall for nine years. The wall has nothing to offer. That's the point. You're not sitting toward something.

---

## Shikantaza: Just This

Dōgen's instruction for zazen is shikantaza — "just sitting," or more literally, "nothing but sitting, wholeheartedly." No object of concentration. No koan. No particular technique. Simply sitting, fully present, without expectation or agenda.

This sounds simpler than samatha or Vipassanā, and in a sense it is. There is no method to learn, no stages to pass through. But it is also, for many practitioners, more difficult — because the mind that wants a method, wants to be doing something right, wants to be making progress, has nothing to grab on to.

Shikantaza is sometimes misunderstood as passive — just sitting, doing nothing. Dōgen was emphatic that this is wrong. The quality of attention required is precisely pointed, fully alive. It is not zoning out. It is the most complete possible presence with each moment as it is, without adding anything or taking anything away.

Dōgen's enigmatic phrase: "To practice the Buddha way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self." Shikantaza is the forgetting of self in the completeness of presence.

## Koan Practice

In Rinzai Zen, practice is organized around koans — the impossible questions and paradoxical statements we explored in Chapter 11.

A student beginning koan practice is typically given the koan "Mu" — from the exchange between the monk who asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature and the master Zhaozhou who answers "Mu." The instruction: become Mu. Don't analyze it. Don't interpret it. Don't look for a clever answer. Sit with it until you and Mu are no longer separate.

What's being asked for is not a conceptual resolution but a kind of collapse — the mind that is trying to solve the koan giving way to something prior to that trying. When this happens, the student presents their understanding to the teacher in *dokusan* (private interview). There is no explaining or justifying. Something is either present or it isn't, and the teacher knows the difference.

Working through a koan curriculum — which can take decades — is said to systematically clean out every corner of the conceptual mind's capacity to generate a false substitute for genuine insight. Each koan that is resolved is another layer of the constructed self that falls away.

## Sesshin

The intensive Zen retreat — *sesshin* — typically lasts five to seven days. The schedule is demanding: wake up around 4 or 5 AM, zazen through the day with breaks for meals and work periods, lights out around 9 or 10 PM. Perhaps eight to twelve hours of zazen per day.

There is little talking. You eat in the meditation hall in a formal practice called *oryoki*. You do work practice — cleaning, cooking, gardening — with the same attention brought to zazen.

The effect of this much sitting, in silence, with no escape into distraction, is hard to describe without having done it. The first days are often uncomfortable — physical pain, restlessness, the mind's resistance to being deprived of its habitual entertainments. By the third or fourth day, something often shifts. The quality of sitting deepens. The gap between meditation and daily activity begins to close. What was two things — meditation and life — starts to become one.

---

## Work Practice

Zen is distinctive in its integration of meditation and work. The great Tang dynasty Chan master Baizhang is credited with the saying: "A day without work is a day without eating." In Zen monasteries, the garden, the kitchen, the maintenance of the buildings are not breaks from practice — they are practice.

This reflects something deep in the Zen understanding of awakening: it is not a special state achieved on the cushion and then lost when you stand up. It is the quality of presence that can be brought to any activity. Washing dishes with full attention is zazen. Chopping vegetables is zazen. The test of genuine Zen is not how you sit in the meditation hall; it's how you are in the rest of your life.

For modern practitioners without access to a monastery, this teaching is both challenging and liberating. Your commute is zazen. Your email is zazen. Washing the dishes is zazen. The question is only: are you present, or are you somewhere else?
