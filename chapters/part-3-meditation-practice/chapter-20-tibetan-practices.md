# Chapter 20: Tibetan Practices

---

Tibetan Buddhist practice is, of all the Buddhist traditions, the most visually and ritually complex. Walking into a Tibetan shrine room for the first time — the thangka paintings, the butter lamps, the elaborate altar offerings, the smell of incense, the sound of Tibetan chanting — can feel like entering a different world entirely.

This complexity is not decoration. Every element carries meaning. Every practice is precisely designed to work on the mind from multiple angles simultaneously — through image, sound, body posture, breath, visualization, and philosophical understanding. The Tibetan tradition developed with the explicit goal of creating maximum conditions for transformation, and that thoroughness is reflected in the sophistication of its methods.

Here we'll focus on practices accessible to lay practitioners — the foundational practices, ngöndro, and the most widely taught meditation methods.

---

## Taking Refuge and Bodhicitta

Every Tibetan Buddhist practice session begins with two foundational orientations: taking refuge and generating bodhicitta.

Taking refuge means orienting toward the Three Jewels — the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha — as one's source of guidance and protection. In the Tibetan context, this is often extended to a fourth refuge: the lama or teacher. You are not going it alone. You are placing yourself within a lineage that stretches back to the Buddha and forward to all those who will be helped by the practice.

Generating bodhicitta — the aspiration to achieve awakening for the benefit of all beings — is the Mahāyāna frame that contextualizes everything else. Before any practice, the practitioner reminds themselves: this is not for me alone. This is for all sentient beings. The motivation transforms the practice.

---

## Ngöndro: The Foundational Practices

Before receiving advanced tantric teachings, Tibetan Buddhist students typically complete the *ngöndro* — "preliminary practices" or "foundational practices." Though called preliminary, they are not superficial; many teachers say the ngöndro is in some ways the most important practice in the tradition.

The ngöndro consists of four practices, each completed 100,000 times:

**Prostrations** — full-length prostrations (lying flat on the floor, arms extended overhead, rising, palms together), accompanied by the visualization of the refuge tree and recitation of the refuge prayer. 100,000 prostrations. This is physical work, often taking years. The practice is a whole-body expression of humility, of acknowledging how much you need help, of releasing the ego's investment in its own grandeur.

**Vajrasattva purification** — visualization of Vajrasattva, the Buddha of purification, above the crown of the head, with nectar flowing down and purifying negative karma. 100,000 recitations of the 100-syllable mantra. This practice works with the recognition that you carry conditioned patterns — habitual reactions, past actions, the weight of self-deception — and that these can be released.

**Mandala offering** — symbolically offering the entire universe — all its beauty, wealth, and goodness — to the lamas and buddhas. 100,000 repetitions. This practice works directly with attachment and the habit of grasping.

**Guru yoga** — merging one's mind with the mind of the teacher, recognizing the teacher as inseparable from the Buddha. This is the heart of the ngöndro, and the practice most directly aimed at opening the capacity for the transmissions that come later.

## Visualization Practice: Deity Yoga

With proper empowerment from a qualified teacher, Tibetan practitioners engage in elaborate visualization practices — *sadhanas* — in which they visualize themselves as a particular Buddha or bodhisattva.

A typical sadhana might take thirty minutes to an hour. It begins with refuge and bodhicitta, moves through the dissolution of ordinary appearance and the arising of the deity's form, includes recitation of mantra (sometimes thousands of repetitions), and concludes with the dissolution of the visualization and dedication of merit.

The visualization is precise and demanding — the deity's form, color, ornaments, implements, posture, throne, retinue must all be held clearly in the mind simultaneously. This is not easy. Students typically spend years building facility with visualization before the practice runs smoothly.

What is the point of imagining yourself as a deity? The tradition's answer: habitual identity is itself a kind of imagination. We spend our lives generating a self-image that we then inhabit as if it were fixed and real. Deity practice deliberately replaces the habitual self-image with an image of awakened qualities — compassion, wisdom, fearlessness — and trains the mind to rest in that identification. Over time, the qualities of the deity begin to express themselves more naturally in daily life.

## Mantra

Tibetan practice makes extensive use of mantra — sacred syllables, phrases, and prayers recited repeatedly, often with a mala (prayer beads) for counting.

The most widely known is *Om mani padme hum* — the mantra of Chenrezig (Avalokiteshvara), the bodhisattva of compassion. Tibetans carve it on stones, spin it in prayer wheels, murmur it through daily activities. Each syllable is associated with a purification — of body, speech, mind, qualities, and activities — and the mantra as a whole is understood as containing the entire Dharma in condensed form.

Mantra works on multiple levels. The repetition stabilizes attention, similar to a samatha object. The sacred syllables are understood to carry blessings from the lineage that has used them. And the meaning — held in mind even when not consciously articulated — infuses the recitation with intention.

---

## Dzogchen: The Direct Approach

For practitioners who have received the appropriate transmissions and have the necessary preparation, Dzogchen offers perhaps the most direct practice in the entire Buddhist tradition.

The practice of *rigpa* — resting in the nature of mind — requires no visualization, no mantra, no elaboration. The teacher introduces the student to the nature of their own awareness — open, luminous, unobstructed, primordially pure — and the student is asked to simply rest there, without distraction or fabrication.

"Without distraction or fabrication" is, of course, precisely what is hard. The mind fabricates constantly — generating thoughts, grasping at states it wants to maintain, pushing away states it dislikes. Dzogchen practice is the training of recognizing these movements without following them, letting them arise and dissolve in the space of awareness that doesn't move.

The famous instruction: "Do not follow the past. Do not anticipate the future. Do not fixate on the present. Rest naturally."

Simple to say. A lifetime to embody.
